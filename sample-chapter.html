Chapter One

The Forest Path

✦ ─────────── ✦

“…help…”

A voice. Female. Barely there. Fraying at the edges like cloth pulled too thin.

Hiranmay Singh’s gaze dropped to the ground before him.

A dark trail cut through the pale dust.

Blood.

———

The forest path lay nearly deserted that evening. The sun had disappeared beneath the horizon, leaving an amber glow across the evening sky. Fireflies flickered among the roots and undergrowth as dusk settled over the forest. The air smelled of dust and dried vegetation, of earth that had forgotten rain. Not a drop had fallen during the previous year’s monsoon. Now, with the month of Bhadrapad upon them, this year’s rains had failed to arrive as well.

Hiranmay Singh moved along the narrow trail at a steady pace, his footsteps heavy with the accumulated weight of a long day at his uncle’s iron workshop in the next village. At nineteen, he carried himself like a man older than his years, broad in the shoulders and muscular through the arms, his skin a deep natural gold, darkened further by the forge heat and open sky. Thick, wavy hair fell to his shoulders, framing a face marked by a faint moustache and striking amber-brown eyes. Those eyes now moved without ceasing, cataloguing the forest on either side of the path the way a watchful animal surveys unfamiliar territory.

The prolonged drought had altered the forest. The water sources had contracted, the prey had thinned and dispersed, and the lions that had once kept largely to the deeper wilderness had grown lean and bold, appearing more frequently along paths where they had no business being. A hungry predator is a different creature from a satisfied one, less patient, less selective, willing to consider options that hunger alone makes thinkable.

He had checked the remaining daylight twice since leaving the gate of his uncle’s workshop, and both times he knew it would run out before he reached home. He was walking faster than the day’s work had left him strength for, but he did not slow down. His faded, soot-stained bagalbandi crossed over his chest and was tied at the side, the cloth forming deep folds at the front. It was marked with dried sweat, its sleeves rolled to the elbows. His dhoti was wrapped firmly around his waist and legs.

Then he heard it.

“…help…”

He stopped abruptly, holding his breath as he listened. His gaze dropped to the ground before him, where a dark trail cut through the pale dust.

Blood.

He followed the trail with his eyes.

And then he saw her.

She lay against the stone in a way that bodies lie when they have stopped choosing their position. Her saree had twisted around her, the fabric bunched and displaced, darkened with blood and dust in patches. The full-sleeved blouse beneath covered her completely, which was the only thing about her situation that retained any dignity. Her face was masked almost wholly in dried mud, cracked where it had begun to flake, streaked in two or three places where tears or sweat or blood had cut through it. Strands of dark hair lay across her cheek, tangled and matted.

Her eyes were half-open.

And yet they were remarkable eyes, wide, shaped with a gentle elongation at the outer corners that reminded him, incongruously, of the lotus leaves that used to cover the lake in better seasons. They were not focused on anything. They trembled faintly as she struggled to keep them open.

One hand lay at her side, the fingers curled loosely. Slender fingers. They seemed to belong to someone who had known a gentler life. Hiranmay stood looking at her. The forest around them had fallen silent.

The image that arrived uninvited was of his parents, the way they had been found, the silence of that place, the finality of blade wounds. He pushed the image down and replaced it with something more manageable: a calculation.

“I’ll leave her near the gates,” he said quietly, to no one, as though saying it made it likelier to be true.

———

“…please…”

The voice again. Fainter now, as though each word cost her the last of her strength.

The calculation he had run a moment ago, the one that had ended with leaving her at the gates, did not survive the sound of her voice. He had run it against an abstract girl in a dark forest. She was not abstract.

He moved.

The goatskin flask was at her lips before he had fully knelt. Water touched her lips, cracked and almost colourless. She stirred. He found her wrist and pressed his thumb where the pulse should be.

It was there. Faint and erratic, like a flame in moving air.

“Still fighting,” he murmured.

The right hand was the worst: a deep, ragged cut, the skin around it swelling. Thorn scratches ran up both arms, suggesting she had forced her way through a jujube bush.

He did not wait further. He transferred his tools from his side to his back and lifted her in a single controlled motion, one arm beneath her shoulders, one beneath her knees. She weighed almost nothing. Less than his tools. Less than a morning’s worth of iron stock. He rose with her easily and began walking.

The forest thinned as he moved north towards Sangrampur, a small gadh in northern Gujarat, close to the border with Rajputana.

The main road announced itself through sound before it was visible: the grind of iron-rimmed wheels on compacted stone, the low exchange of voices and the rhythmic jingle of harness bells.

Merchants.

Hiranmay slowed without stopping and angled himself into the shadow of a stunted tree. Through its branches, he glimpsed the lanterns of the approaching carts.

He looked down at the girl in his arms. Unconscious. Bloodied. Barely breathing. If anyone on that road saw him carrying her at this hour, in this state, the conversation that followed would be long and dangerous and would end in the wrong place for everyone involved.

He stepped away from the road and into the farmland. The ground changed underfoot, ridged and uneven, with dried irrigation channels running between rows of struggling crops. Some patches remained faintly green, while others had dried out completely.

Hiranmay knew these fields the way he knew his own hands. He moved through them at a pace that looked leisurely and covered ground quickly, following paths that would have been invisible to anyone who had not grown up knowing them.

A voice came from the shadows ahead.

“Who’s there?”

A figure rose from a low wooden platform built for watching over the crops at night. He had a stout stick in hand and the alertness of a man who had been dozing lightly a moment before.

Nanji kaka.

He stepped forward, narrowing his eyes against the fading light. “Hiranmay?”

“What are you carrying?”

Hiranmay held his position. “Someone injured, kaka. Found her near the lake road.”

Nanji kaka moved closer, trying to make out the figure in Hiranmay’s arms. “He Bhagwan! Injured. At this hour. Coming from that direction.”

“I was going to put her outside the town gates.”

The older man was quiet for a moment, his stick still raised.

“These are not good times,” Nanji kaka said finally. “Be careful with what you carry.”

Nanji kaka lowered his voice. “A delegation arrived from the provincial court today. New officials. Border tensions, they’re saying. More patrols, more questions about who is moving and why.” He glanced at the unconscious girl. “You walk into the town gates looking like that and they’ll hold you until morning at the earliest. Possibly longer.”

Hiranmay said nothing, but his grip on the girl shifted slightly as he reconsidered his next move.

“Take her to the farmhouse,” Nanji kaka said. “The barn. We need your kaki.”

“Saaru.”

The farmhouse was set back from the edge of the field, just on the outskirts of the town, its outline dim against the sky. The barn beside it smelled of dry hay and old timber. Hiranmay stepped inside, waited for his eyes to adjust, then moved towards the charpoy against the far wall.

He reached the charpoy but did not lower her immediately. Supporting her with one arm, he cleared the loose debris from its surface. Then he removed his pagri, the worn head-wrap, and spread it over the rough rope so that her wounds would not rest against it. Only then did he lower her onto it, taking care not to aggravate her injuries.

Her breathing remained shallow. Fragile.

Nanji kaka had followed him in. He watched as Hiranmay carefully settled the girl onto the charpoy. Without a word, he removed the shawl from his own shoulders and draped it over her, covering her from chin to feet.

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

Then Hiranmay straightened. “I’ll get kaki.”

“I’ll watch over her,” Nanji kaka said.

Hiranmay stepped back out into the night, moving faster now towards the lane and the two small faces waiting for him at home.
